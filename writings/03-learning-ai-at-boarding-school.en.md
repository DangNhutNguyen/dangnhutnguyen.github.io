# Learning AI at Boarding School, Where Even the Internet Had a Curfew

*Logbook 03 · The boarding-school years · 2023–2026*

A two-hour video is not really two hours long when I do not know when I will be able to open the computer again. During high school, I lived and studied in a boarding-school environment. A computer, an Internet connection, and private time were not always available when I wanted them. Most programming and AI material, meanwhile, assumes the learner can sit at a machine, open ten tabs, run the code, and search for an error immediately. That was not the rhythm I had. Some days I could read a great deal but run nothing. Sometimes I downloaded a document and had no time to open it. A simple error could remain in my head until the next time I touched a computer. Learning did not happen in one long session. It came in pieces. I do not believe limited conditions automatically make someone more disciplined. Often, they are simply frustrating. Without a machine, a false hypothesis could survive much longer. Without the Internet at the right moment, I could remain stuck on a concept that might have taken five minutes to explain. Interrupted sessions broke the thread of a project, and every return required time just to remember where I had been. Better conditions are still better. The right tools still matter. But because I could not change the situation immediately, I had to change the way I learned.

I began downloading material in advance. I saved the videos I needed. Long documentation became a list of sections to read instead of a page I opened at random and hoped to remember. Unanswered questions went into a notebook so that, when the Internet returned, I could search for them in order. More importantly, I separated learning into two kinds. The first required a computer: running code, debugging, installing libraries, and checking results. The second did not always require one: reading equations, drawing the data flow, predicting tensor shapes, writing pseudocode, or explaining a concept to myself. Before that, I treated the second kind as “preparation,” while coding was the real learning. Later I realized that many errors would have disappeared if I had first agreed to draw the system on paper. A notebook cannot run Python. It can, however, expose the place where my reasoning quietly jumps over a step. When learning from the Internet, I find it very easy to collect material. One Python course, three machine-learning playlists, several “awesome” repositories, and another mathematics course. The list grows, and I begin to feel that I am building a serious library. The library does not turn itself into understanding.

With little time, I had to change the unit of learning from “resource” to “question.” For example: why is an activation function necessary? what ability does the model lose without a bias term? in which direction is the gradient moving through this layer? why is the program using more memory than I predicted? One question may require several sources. A long course, in contrast, may answer only the small part I need. When time arrives in fragments, learning by question is more realistic than the ambition to “finish everything.” There was one advantage I understood only later. When I could not search immediately, I had to remain with a question for longer. I did not always discover the answer. Most of the time, I did not. But I began to form the habit of predicting. When I finally ran the experiment, the result was no longer only right or wrong. It became a comparison with the model in my head. That is also why I still like learning mathematics and physics beside code. They give me a place to think without requiring every idea to execute immediately. At university, I gained more control over my time. The paradox is that I also became easier to distract. The Internet no longer has a curfew, so it can occupy an entire day. Material is no longer scarce. What is scarce is a reason clear enough to ignore everything else.

I am trying to keep a few habits from boarding school: choose one main question before opening the browser; record the state of a project so the next session does not begin from zero; download and organize important material instead of depending on one open tab; reserve some time for thinking on paper; end a session by asking, “What is the next small step?” These habits do not make me permanently disciplined. I still have days when I open the material and do something else, or create a plan more beautiful than the hours I actually study. But at least I know how to return. Learning under limitations did not give me a story in which effort alone is enough. Effort does not replace a computer, an Internet connection, or a mentor. I moved slowly through many parts because I lacked them. What I gained was the ability to hold a question without an answer and not abandon it too quickly. If I continue into AI research, perhaps that is exactly the ability I will need. Most research does not look like the final model running successfully. It looks more like an evening when I know something is wrong but do not yet have enough evidence to know where. At boarding school, the Internet had a curfew. Fortunately, curiosity did not.
