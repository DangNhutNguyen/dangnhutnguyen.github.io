# Children’s Forum

*Logbook 01 · 12 August 2019 · written in September 2020*

“I honestly think I should have posted this story sooner, instead of waiting all the way until now. Seven years since the first time I set foot in Hanoi-when I experienced almost everything new, almost everything most special in my journey.”

The innocent parts: There were some very special things I was capable of, or perhaps some very special chances that came within my reach. Sometimes they came thanks to my mother, thanks to people in my family. I was given “special admission” to the provincial Children’s Forum-which explains why I had never gone through the district-level selection (back when it was still the old Đất Đỏ District), yet somehow skipped a large stage of selection to arrive at a Forum that people considered “truly useless” for children today. It was understandable enough: who in their right mind would listen to a speech from a schoolboy who had not even made it all the way through the doors of primary school? “Was there someone behind him, holding him up?” No! My mother is a teacher, my father is a real farmer; I have always insisted on this-to turn it into something that pushes me forward. No connection in my family was ever important enough for me to be “allowed” to say things that did not belong to the thoughts of a real child... I had prepared a “very long” speech, almost an accusation against a way of operating that was disorganized yet genuinely influential in the lives of us children. “A forum that listens to children speak”-“does it really listen?” I am sure so much had already gone in one ear and out the other that even I could no longer feel it happening.

Oh, lucky me! It had been a long day. Two weeks later, I received a call from the Committee for Youth and Children-with an invitation to attend the 6th National Children’s Forum. Was I excited? Yes!-But? I had never fully believed in my own ability-simply because I was still young enough not to recognize the benefits, or sometimes the effects those things might have on me. Those sleepless summer days just kept rushing past, through two months. 12 August 2019 I stepped out of the house with a suitcase that was “completely empty!?” No-it had everything in it, but to me it was still empty in the end, because it was empty enough for me to store all the memories that would become so deep, perhaps when the day came that I had grown up. Before that, from the little experience I had, I had come to know many members of the UNICEF Vietnam council and the Committee for Child Safety, together with many hopes from the people involved; I had pushed aside all the “mockery” that, I was told, made this Forum useless. Could those voices-actually be heard? Could those forum models-be useful for anything?

Let’s get on the plane! A small delay, with 45 minutes spent waiting at the airport. No rush! That was also where a little flicker of passion began to glow in me. I used to be a child deeply interested in engineering-but all the things I had heard, had seen, had wanted somehow pulled down a thin curtain, and behind it appeared the word “Pilot.” What was I waiting for? Weren’t pilots those tall, “handsome” men and uncles? Would I be good enough to take even the first step! Without one more moment of thought, there it was-once again, I wanted to change my future job, to change my dream. After two hours staring closely at layers of clouds, I set foot in Nội Bài Airport. A breath of heat kept blowing until my whole body felt hot. But perhaps it was also setting fire to a restless flame inside me. What would the following days be like? Where would I get to go and have fun? What should I do? Did I truly deserve to have my name placed beside the other council members? Drugs-Fragility

I was assigned to a group and given the name “the smallest child in the whole room”-I had never thought I could be that steady when I found myself in a place where: Everyone was an adult. The youngest person I came to know in the group was already 18 and “had once been a child from an SOS Children’s Village.” My comments on the issue and on how we could approach it were finally accepted into “one box” on the mind map of things that needed to be said. I have never tried drugs-certainly. But my view often came from listening to the harm they caused children through the echoes left by people those children loved. It turned out that drugs did not always touch the children directly-but reached them through the people they had once called family. Those fathers and mothers seemed never to have thought about the effect they had on their own children. Or perhaps it was simply the answer to the question: “Can Mom and Dad stay close to me?” Very difficult! And when we look back-have we ever really seen ourselves from the other side, as a son or daughter, as the younger one in a family? What had those children done to deserve any of it? We considered many different cases around this issue. We placed blame, too, on most stories like those.

The fragile bonds inside a family continued to fray; the fragile future of the children, the fragile future of the self, kept being carried off by the wind, away from fathers, mothers, and families that had not yet heard the echo. It really did fly! It flew like a family that had already left! But... Had we ever really understood? What follows may blow away all the gravity that seems to remain there forever: perhaps this is only my view-perhaps mine alone, perhaps it is about “leaving a road open” for those families. I understand that they made trade-offs; sometimes none of it was what they wanted, and everything began as chance-simple mistakes they abandoned too quickly, moments when responsibility slipped from their hands, silences no one named in time. Perhaps, in some corner of it all, we were only trying to touch a truth that was very soft and very painful: people do not always choose correctly, but the cost of a wrong choice so often falls upon shoulders far too small.

And I asked myself-is that precisely what makes drugs even more frightening? Not only because they ruin a body, a life, but because they can turn a home into a foreign place. They do not only take one person away. They also drag away the way a child sees their father, their mother; the way a younger sibling calls the name of a brother or sister; the way a family once believed they might still be able to hold on to one another. I stood in that group, among adults, among stories that seemed to have grown calloused after being told too many times, but were in truth still soaked through on the inside. There were moments when I felt very small. But there were also moments when I understood that being small did not make my voice weaker. On the contrary, it made that voice more real. Because I was not speaking from the distance of someone who had lived through everything. I was speaking from the position of someone who had seen how everything first began to crack.

And perhaps what we wanted to say was not only “stay away from drugs.” It went much deeper than that. Look at the children before everything becomes too late. Look at the way one careless act by an adult can become an entire season casting its shadow over a childhood. Look at those tiny hands before they have to learn how to pick up the pieces someone else left behind. Because in the end, the most fragile thing is not only an addiction. It is trust. It is family. It is the feeling that no matter how fierce the wind becomes outside, home will still be a place to which we can return. Hahaha, where would those sounds, those proposals, those voices go? I simply placed my stories in a corner, then sent them entirely toward the place where I truly believed they ought to go. One year later, those statements and those echoes were used to help change the laws for children and minors. We could be very happy that, at last, we had contributed something; at the most basic level: those were the profound things we should have done, had needed to do. But after all of it, what I remember most is not only the applause, the nods, or the written memoranda left on paper.

What I remember most is a very strange feeling: the feeling that, for the first time, a child could stand among adults and say what they saw, what they thought, what hurt them-and not be treated as meaningless. Because children do not only need to be “heard” in the way adults often say. Children need to be protected before they have time to learn how to protect themselves. They need to be kept safe before the first wounds have time to leave their marks. They need to be recognized as human beings who are growing up, rather than little shadows that can be overlooked simply because they are not yet old enough to make their voices perfectly clear. I once thought that perhaps adults always knew what was best for children. But the more stories I passed through, the more lives I listened to, the more I understood that adults are not always right. There are silences from parents that wound their children. There are habits inside families that push children into very deep cracks. There are things that appear to be “adult matters” but are the entire world of a child. And there are very small things-a flash of anger, one time being left alone, one moment of being ignored-that are enough to bend someone’s childhood out of shape forever.

Protecting children, then, is not only writing laws. It is not only putting up slogans. And it is certainly not organizing eloquent conversations, then letting everything continue as before. Protecting children means placing responsibility exactly where it belongs. It means requiring adults to look again at how they treat their children, their younger siblings, their students, and even the children society so often overlooks without meaning to. It means creating a system serious enough that when a child speaks, they do not have to scream at the top of their lungs before someone believes them. I think about that often. I think about homes that appear peaceful but are filled inside with invisible scratches. I think about children growing up in fear, in absence, in an insecurity they did not create. I think about children who never chose how their lives would begin, yet have to live with the consequences of other people’s choices. And I see that if society truly wants to protect children, it must first learn to respect the immaturity of childhood. To respect even the things they have not yet put into words. To respect even the fears they do not yet know how to name. Because children do not need promises that are too large. They need a hand safe enough to hold. An adult kind enough to trust. A mechanism fair enough to shelter them. And a world civilized enough not to force them to grow up through trauma.

Perhaps, many years later, people will forget the names of some of the children who spoke that day. Perhaps they will even forget our very long, very clumsy, very honest sentences. But I believe that what comes from the truth does not disappear. It only moves quietly into some place in society’s consciousness, then one day returns as law, as policy, as change, as a safer door for children who have not yet been born or have not yet learned what it means to be afraid. And if there is one thing I want to keep after all of it, it is the belief that children are not objects for adults to pity. They are human beings who need to be protected through real responsibility. Not through words chosen to sound beautiful. But through action strong enough that no child has to grow up in loneliness, in abandonment, or inside wounds no one can see. Because in the end, a society is measured by how it treats its children. And a family is measured by how safe a child can feel inside their own home. September 2020.
