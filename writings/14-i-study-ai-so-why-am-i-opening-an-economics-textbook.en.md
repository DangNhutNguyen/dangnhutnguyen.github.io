# I Study AI, So Why Am I Opening an Economics Textbook?

*Logbook 14 · August 2026*

After entering the Artificial Intelligence program, I started looking for textbooks on microeconomics, macroeconomics, finance, and the stock market. The reasonable response is: finish the degree you already chose first. I agree with half of that. I do not want to study economics just to add another list of subjects, and I do not think reading a few books is equivalent to earning a second university degree. The real reason is that I increasingly see that AI does not operate in a vacuum. A model predicts, recommends, or automates a decision-and every decision exists in a system of incentives, risks, and people. If I only understand the model, I might become very good at optimizing the wrong objective. When I tried building an MVP for analyzing Vietnamese stocks, the data and the model were the exciting parts at first. There were time-series prices, indicators, features, backtests, and a question that sounded very much like machine learning: will it go up or down tomorrow? Very quickly, however, I ran into questions that could not be solved simply by changing the model: does a correct prediction still produce a profit after costs? what are the risks of the sequence of decisions? does the data contain survivorship bias or leakage? when the market changes regime, does the old model still mean anything? does the user need a probability, a signal, or a limit on how much they can lose?

Accuracy is not P&L. And P&L is not yet a good strategy if the risk makes the strategy impossible to survive. This was when I realized that domain knowledge is not something to "add on" after the AI has been built. It determines the label, the metric, the baseline, and whether the problem is worth solving in the first place. Microeconomics asks about choice under scarcity, incentives, and how agents respond. Macroeconomics looks at growth, inflation, policy, and movements at the scale of a system. Finance brings time, risk, and uncertainty into value. Those questions sit very close to modern AI systems: how does a recommender system change user behavior, and then change the data it will receive in the future? what incentives does an algorithm that optimizes engagement create for the platform? when many agents use the same model, does the advantage disappear? whose costs does automation reduce, and to whom does it transfer risk? can a prediction that is good on average harm a smaller group? AI can be very good at recognizing patterns. Economics reminds me that once people know a system is predicting them, they may change their behavior. Data is not only observed. It is also created by the rules of the game. I used to have a habit of downloading an entire curriculum, dividing it into beautifully named folders, and calling that a roadmap. This time, I want to be more careful. I divide the learning into three layers.

Microeconomics, macroeconomics, accounting principles, probability and statistics, and the time value of money. The goal is to read an argument and recognize where its main assumptions are hiding. Corporate finance, investing, risk management, econometrics, and causal inference. This is the clearest bridge to data science. Analyze a company, test a strategy against a serious baseline, study the effect of a policy, or build a tool that helps users understand risk. A project should force me to use the concepts, not merely display charts. I do not yet know whether I will pursue a formal second program or continue through self-study. But either way, I want outputs that can test what I know: essays, reproducible notebooks, small projects, and a record of the times my predictions were wrong. Knowing how to program creates the feeling that I can move quickly in a new domain. Just get the data, call an API, draw a dashboard, and train a model. A demo can appear before I understand what the data represents. In finance, that is especially dangerous, because a beautiful backtest curve can be produced very easily by leakage, overfitting, or one favorable period.

I want to keep a few rules: do not use the test set to choose a strategy and still call it a test; always compare against a simple baseline; include transaction costs and liquidity constraints where appropriate; report drawdown and uncertainty, not only profit; do not turn a learning experiment into investment advice; when I do not understand a financial product, do not let the model give me the feeling that I do. The more confident the model sounds, the more its builder needs to know when to say, "I don't know." I do not see AI and economics as two fields from which I have to choose one. AI gives me tools to model, predict, and build systems. Economics gives me the language to understand the objectives, incentives, and effects of those systems. Their intersection might be fintech, market design, policy, risk, AI for decision-making, or a direction whose name I do not know yet. I do not need to decide now. What I need is not to become someone who is very good at optimizing a metric but does not know whose life that metric is changing. So beside my AI notebook, I still open an economics textbook. Not to leave the field I chose. But to know what kind of world the models I build will eventually enter.
