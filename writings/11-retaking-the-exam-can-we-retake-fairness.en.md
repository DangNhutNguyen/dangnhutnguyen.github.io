# Retaking the Exam - Can We Retake Fairness?

*Logbook 11 · Summer 2026*

“Make everyone retake the exam” sounds like a Reset button. The results are in doubt? Administer another test. The old scores are questioned? Produce new ones. If everyone starts again, fairness seems to return to the starting line as well. But people are not programs that can be restarted in the same state. An exam paper can be printed again. The original day cannot. Imagine a student entering the first exam after months of preparation, with a particular state of mind and health on that day. Later, the student is told to retake it because of a failure they did not cause. From that moment, the conditions have changed. They may have more time to prepare-or no time left at all. They may remember the parts they did poorly. They may carry pressure from the public, their family, and the fear that whatever the new score says, other people will still look at them with suspicion. Some students may already have moved on to university applications, traveled elsewhere, or begun a different plan. The second exam no longer measures the student who entered the first room. It measures the student after a disruption. That does not make a retake meaningless in every case. But if we treat the new score as a perfect substitute for the old one, we ignore the largest change of all: the person taking the exam has gone through an experience the system cannot erase.

A policy is often called fair when it applies the same rule to everyone. Its consequences, however, may be very different. A student who can continue studying enters the second exam differently from one who must work, travel, or no longer has access to the same learning environment. A student deeply affected by the pressure is not in the same position as someone who sees the retake as a chance to improve. A high-scoring student has more to lose than a student who wanted another attempt. The same rule does not automatically impose the same cost. That is why “everyone will retake it” feels like an answer given too quickly to a complicated problem. It may be easier to organize than identifying what actually failed and who was genuinely affected. But convenience for the system should not be confused with fairness for the students. In my analyses of examination data, I used statistics to ask how rare a distribution was, compare similar groups, inspect clusters of candidate numbers, and simulate the effects of different policy choices. Those tools are useful. They force the discussion to move beyond intuition. Yet every simulation requires an assumption about a student’s “true ability,” something I cannot directly observe. If we place greater trust in the original scores, preserving them may minimize error. If we believe the original scores were heavily affected, scaling or retesting may look better under the chosen metric.

Change the assumption, and the “optimal” policy may change with it. A table of results therefore cannot declare which policy is fair. It can only say: if we believe these assumptions and measure harm in this way, these are the consequences we should expect. The rest is a value judgment. Which type of mistake is unacceptable? What evidence is strong enough to alter someone’s educational opportunity? Who should carry the risk when the system remains uncertain? There is a strong temptation to create a new score column so that the records look consistent again. But a clean number does not prove a clean process. If the original exam contains signals that require verification, the first responsibilities are to preserve the evidence, make the review method transparent, separate statistical signals from investigative proof, and give affected students the right to know, explain, and appeal. A policy should be evaluated by more than the average score after adjustment. It should also answer: how many innocent students might lose an opportunity? how many students who benefited unfairly might remain undetected? which groups bear a heavier cost under the same rule? does the conclusion change sharply under other reasonable assumptions? does the policy create a precedent that could be misused later? Without those questions, a retake merely transfers uncertainty from the institution to the students.

No option can perfectly restore an exam that has lost public trust. Keeping the scores may preserve an error. Canceling them may harm people who had nothing to do with the problem. Scaling depends on a model. A retake creates a measurement under new conditions. Admitting that there is no clean answer is not a refusal to decide. It is the beginning of a more honest decision. My central principle is that an individual should not bear the entire cost of a system failure they did not create. If decision-makers must choose among imperfect options, they should publish their assumptions, measure more than one form of harm, build protections for affected students, and take responsibility for the uncertainty that remains. An examination can be organized again. The time, mindset, opportunity, and trust of the students cannot be returned to the original day. So before asking, “Should they retake the exam?”, perhaps we need to ask the harder question: Who is being given the chance to correct a mistake-and who is being asked to pay for that correction?
