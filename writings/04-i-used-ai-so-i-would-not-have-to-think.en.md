# I Used AI So I Wouldn’t Have to Think

*Logbook 04 · Around 2023–2024*

There was a period when I used AI a great deal and learned very little. From the outside, everything looked fine. I had code that ran, explanations that sounded reasonable, and a long list of “next steps.” When I met an error, I pasted it into a chat window. When I did not know how to design the system, I asked. When I needed to write a section of a report, a draft appeared a few seconds later. The problem was simple: answers were arriving faster than I could understand the questions. AI is very good at making me feel as though I am only one prompt away from the solution. It suggests a library, so I install it. It rewrites a function, and the error disappears. It gives me an architecture that looks clean, so I carry it into the project. In the short term, this becomes strangely addictive because every press of Enter produces something new. Then the pieces begin to disagree, and I no longer know which one to trust. A new suggestion does not remember the assumptions behind an earlier decision. A patch fixes the current error and quietly breaks something elsewhere. At one point, I could not explain why the system worked except with the most honest answer available: “AI told me to do it this way.” That was when I realized I had borrowed confidence from a tool.

The confidence lasted until the project moved beyond familiar examples on the Internet. From there, the person who had to judge a hypothesis, choose a trade-off, and live with the result was still me. Without a model of the problem in my own head, I could not even recognize where the answer was wrong. I did not wake up one morning and suddenly forget how to think. It happened through small choices: reading the answer instead of the original documentation; running code before predicting what it would do; accepting a term because it sounded correct; asking AI to divide the problem before trying to write the problem down myself; fixing tests until they turned green without asking what those tests actually proved. Each choice saved a few minutes. Together, they removed the most important part of learning: staying with the difficulty long enough to build a model of my own. I do not believe that struggle is always noble. Some installation errors are meaningless. Some syntax problems do not deserve an entire afternoon, and AI handles them well. But not every form of friction should be removed. Some friction is exactly where understanding begins. After several months, I stopped and looked at what I had built. The project had many pieces, but I did not understand the whole. The notes contained many solutions but very few hypotheses. I had optimized for producing output, not for learning. So I began again with a clearer goal.

This time, before asking AI, I wrote three lines: what do I know? what am I only assuming? what result would make me change my mind? If I needed code, I tried to write a small version first. If AI proposed a method, I looked for its documentation or original source. If I had two options, I stopped asking the general question “Which is better?” and wrote down the actual criterion: easier to test, lower memory use, or a better fit for the data? AI was still there. It was simply no longer in the driver’s seat. I do not have a perfect process, but a few rules have helped. Before executing code, I try to state its input, output, and at least one way it could fail. A wrong prediction is still useful; it gives me something to compare with reality. AI can suggest search terms, challenge an approach, or explain a concept differently. The final decision needs a reason I can defend without borrowing the model’s confidence. For a complicated pipeline, I want a baseline simple enough to understand completely. It may perform worse, but it tells me whether the complicated part contributes anything real. If I close the AI window and cannot explain the project to someone else, I do not yet own the knowledge. A log containing only successful results is a story edited too aggressively. Failed hypotheses show where learning took place.

I do not want this story to become a warning to abandon AI. I study artificial intelligence, build with it, and hope to research the technology itself. It would be rather strange if my conclusion were simply, “Do not use it.” The better question is: which part of this process must I refuse to give away? For me, it is the work of asking the question, defining the evidence, and taking responsibility for the decision. AI can shorten the distance to an answer. It should not shorten that distance so much that understanding is skipped entirely. I used to be afraid of a project that ran slowly. Now I am more afraid of a project that runs while the person who built it does not know why. Perhaps that is the hardest bug AI ever helped me create.
