# Second Prize in Physics, and Why I Still Chose AI

*Logbook 09 · 2026*

In grade 12, I chose Physics as my main subject for the excellent-student competition. It was not the obvious choice if you looked at the university major I wanted to pursue: Artificial Intelligence. A choice that looked more "on track" might have been to spend all my time on programming, algorithms, or another AI project. I still chose Physics, and later won Second Prize in the city-level excellent-student examination in 2026. Every time I tell this story, it is easy to write it as a turn: from Physics to AI. But I do not feel as if I moved from one world into another. I only changed the kind of system I was trying to understand. Competitive Physics comes with a lot of formulas. If you can remember them quickly enough, sometimes you can still solve the familiar problems. But once a problem changes the way it is asked, a formula left standing on its own becomes fairly helpless. What is more useful is knowing how to ask: which quantity is being conserved? what factor has the model left out? which frame of reference makes the problem simpler? does the result make sense in its units and in intuition? what do the boundary cases say about the solution? Those questions followed me into AI.

What is an impressive accuracy score worth if the model learned from leaked data? Is an improvement real if it only works on one test set? What is a pipeline with many complicated layers actually modeling, or is it only accumulating errors? In both fields, no formula can rescue a wrong assumption. The clearest bridge between Physics and AI for me appeared in a solar-energy project in 2023. While developing a system that could recognize and track a light source, I could not treat recognition, control, and the physical mechanism as three separate projects. The model's prediction eventually had to become movement. If the system oscillated, the cause could be image noise, control logic, latency, or the mechanics. Improving recognition accuracy would not necessarily fix it. That was a very physical lesson: the behavior we observe is the result of the whole system, not just the component we chose because it happened to be the easiest one to measure. Later, while learning about neural networks, I also liked looking at a model as a dynamical system: parameters changing with each optimization step, gradients moving through layers, one small adjustment accumulating into a large behavior. The comparison is not perfect. But it gives me an intuition from which to begin. Winning Second Prize is a milestone I value. It recognized months of practice, wrong solutions, and the choice to stay with a subject whose name did not appear directly in the name of my future major.

But the competition also showed me the limits of learning toward a short destination. When the exam ended, the structure that had made every day so clear suddenly disappeared. There was no longer a list of problem types, a practice schedule, or an examination day to count down to. If the reward was the only reason for learning, this would be where the knowledge stopped. I wanted to carry the way Physics taught me to think somewhere else: modeling a situation before calculating it; checking whether a result is reasonable; being patient with a problem when I do not yet know where to begin; distinguishing a useful model from the reality it approximates. Those things do not expire after the paper is submitted. I chose Artificial Intelligence at UIT because I want to go deeper into how models learn, especially LLM architectures and training efficiency. My long-term goal is to become a research engineer or researcher, and then continue to graduate study if I am capable of it. That direction requires mathematics, code, computer systems, and many skills I do not yet have. It also requires the ability to look at a model without confusing it for the world. Physics gave me a good place to begin.

I do not think interdisciplinary learning means placing two labels side by side and waiting for an idea to appear. Knowledge truly intersects only when a way of thinking in one field helps me ask better questions in another. For me, a Physics question often begins with: what forces and effects are acting on this system? The corresponding AI question might be: how was this result produced by the data, the architecture, the optimization objective, and the evaluation process? People often like stories with a clear turn: once a Physics student, now an AI student. That version is neat, but it is not quite true. I am still the person who once sat for a very long time with a mechanics problem, who built a device that followed a light source, and who still finds it beautiful when one model can explain many phenomena with few assumptions. AI did not replace that part. It gave me a new set of questions. Second Prize in Physics is not evidence that I chose the wrong major. It is a reminder that before I learned how to make machines predict, I learned how to ask the world why.
