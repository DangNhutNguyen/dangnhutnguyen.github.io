# The Day I Became an Artificial Intelligence Student at UIT

*Logbook 13 · August 2026*

The name of my major now includes the words "Artificial Intelligence." It sounds rather official. It appears on my records, in introductions, and in questions like, "What will you do in the future?" But on the day I became a first-year student at UIT – VNU-HCM, I did not suddenly understand transformers more deeply, write cleaner code, or know exactly where the next four years would lead. I only had a new starting point, together with more freedom to decide for myself than before. University does not unlock knowledge like a game. It opens a larger map and removes some of the fences. In high school, most short-term questions had already been answered by someone else: which chapter to study, when the exam would be, which assignment had to be submitted. Even while teaching myself AI, I was still placing it inside the rather tight system of boarding school, examinations, and university-admission goals. University is different because I can remain busy all the time and still move no closer to what I want. There are too many reasonable choices: build solid foundations in mathematics and programming; join a club; make projects; find a lab; practice algorithms; improve my English; read papers; prepare for an internship; think about studying abroad or graduate school. If I turn all of them into my number-one priority, all I have created is a very academic to-do list.

So the first question of university life is not, "How much can I do?" It is, "Which core ability do I need to build first?" While preparing the handwritten essay for the Talent Program, I chose the title "Rough Drafts in the Age of AI." It came from a contradiction I had encountered: an AI tool can produce an almost perfectly clean answer at once, while my real learning is covered in crossed-out lines. There are projects that have to be rebuilt, hypotheses that turn out to be wrong, code that runs even when I do not understand why, and questions that remain longer than I wanted them to. Writing by hand on one sheet of A4 paper meant I could not keep adding ideas forever, move paragraphs around, or ask a tool to smooth everything out. Every sentence had to matter enough to take up space. I do not think handwriting is nobler than technology. But it reminds me that a draft is valuable not because it is imperfect. It is valuable because it leaves behind the trace of choices I made for myself. That is also what I want to keep while studying AI: to use powerful tools, but not hand over the right to ask the question. Honestly, a first-year student should not pretend to understand the entire field they have chosen.

I know AI is not only calling an API or training a model. It rests on mathematics, probability, optimization, data structures, computer systems, and experimental evaluation. A good model also has to be placed inside a product, hardware, data, and the lives of real people. I also know that the part I care about most right now may change. At the moment, I like LLMs, model architecture, and parameter optimization under limited resources. Four years from now, the specific question may be different. What I want to keep is not a keyword. It is a kind of work: understanding a system deeply, designing experiments, and turning the result into something that can be used. I do not want to write a four-year plan detailed down to every month. I have made enough plans to know that their accuracy falls very quickly after the first page. For the first year, I am keeping four principles. Mathematics, Python, DSA, systems, and writing are easy to neglect when a new AI demo looks more exciting. I want enough patience to learn the things that do not immediately produce a beautiful screenshot. A project turns knowledge into a concrete question. "Learn machine learning" is very broad; "build a baseline, measure its errors, and explain three failure cases" is somewhere I can begin.

A blog, a research log, and a README are not only for building a profile. Writing is how I find the places where I thought I understood something but could not explain it. Self-learning has taken me quite far, but it has also given me many blind spots. The value of university lies in lecturers, classmates, research groups, and conversations no playlist can replace. I used to look at students who already had papers, internships, or large projects and feel that I was falling behind. The internet is very efficient at placing my first chapter beside someone else's tenth. But university is not where I come to prove that I already know everything. If it were, enrolling would be rather unnecessary. I came to UIT with a few projects, a foundation in Physics, many plans, and quite a few gaps as well. That does not make me less suited to be here. It only makes the work ahead clearer. The day I became an Artificial Intelligence student was not the moment I completed an old version of myself. It felt more like opening a new notebook. The first page is still a draft.
