# Rough Drafts in the Age of AI

*Logbook 12 · August 2026*

The rough drafts that led me to AI did not begin in a computer science class. In high school, I joined my first research projects on converting energy from sunlight. In early 2023, while developing a system that could detect and track a light source, I watched a few lines of code make a physical mechanism move. For a moment, the boundary between the computer and the physical world became thinner. AI was no longer something that returned an answer on a screen. It could observe, model, and take part in solving a real problem. That was when I began to think seriously about becoming a research engineer-and, if I eventually become capable enough, an AI researcher. But that path did not begin with a finished page. It began with a great many rough drafts. My first project forced me to move across several layers: code, electronics, mechanics, and the problem of testing a complete system. For a while, I left behind one unfinished design after another. The approach kept changing. Each part looked reasonable by itself, but the parts did not form a consistent whole. I turned to AI for help with code and debugging. At first, I thought that if I followed its instructions for long enough, the project would somehow complete itself. AI always had one more answer. That was precisely why it took me so long to notice that the system was drifting away from its original purpose.

After several months, I looked back and realized that the missing piece was not a more powerful tool. I lacked the patience to stay with the problem and the independent judgment to say, “This suggestion does not fit,” even when it was delivered with complete confidence. So I started again. This time, I drew the system as separate blocks. I wrote down what each block should receive, what it should return, and how it might fail. I still used AI, but its role had changed: it could suggest, challenge, and help me find what needed verification. It no longer decided what the system should become. That change mattered more than any individual line of code I fixed. To me, learning AI is not merely calling an API, running a notebook, or recognizing the name of the newest model. A learner needs to understand how a model learns from data, build a foundation in mathematics and computer science, know what a result is actually measuring, and take responsibility for the technology they create. A model can reach high accuracy while learning the wrong signal. An answer can sound fluent while having no evidence behind it. A project can run even when the person who built it cannot explain why.

If the only goal is output, AI makes everything look faster. If the goal is capability, I sometimes need to slow myself down on purpose: predict before running, build a simple baseline, trace a method back to its source, and keep a record of the hypotheses that failed. AI can shorten repetitive work. It should not remove the productive struggle required to understand. When I applied to the Honors Bachelor’s Program in Computer Science at UIT, I did not see the word “talent” as confirmation that I was already good enough. I saw it as a reminder of the responsibility to train more seriously. Such a program should not simply mean taking more courses. What I was looking for was the chance to study more deeply: a strong foundation, advanced knowledge, higher standards, guidance from experienced lecturers, and an environment where students can begin research early. The direction I want to pursue is large language models, especially architectures and training methods that can work efficiently under limited computing resources. It is a long-term goal. I will not reach it by chasing every model that becomes popular or collecting as many course certificates as possible. I need to learn how to ask a testable question, read a paper beyond its abstract, design an experiment with a baseline, and accept a result that does not support what I hoped to find.

If the opportunity exists and I become capable enough, I want to continue into graduate study and contribute to AI systems that are more reliable and better suited to the language, resources, and needs of Vietnam. But a long-term goal still begins with today’s work. AI helped accelerate my project. It also helped me create the feeling of progress without the understanding that should have accompanied it. That is why I do not want to tell this story in either extreme: AI saved me, or AI ruined my work. A tool does not choose its own position in the learning process. The user does. There are three things I do not want to delegate: choosing which question is worth pursuing; deciding what evidence is sufficient to believe an answer; taking responsibility for the answer and the product built from it. AI can produce a draft in seconds. It can make the language smoother and the code appear faster. But it does not live with the consequences of a bad decision in the way the decision-maker does. In an age when a finished answer can appear too easily, perhaps an important ability is keeping the right to write the first rough line ourselves. That line may be ugly. It may be crossed out later. But it is where I can see my own thinking begin to take shape. And where I can tell who the question truly belongs to.
