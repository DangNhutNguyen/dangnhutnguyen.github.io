# An Outlier Is Not Evidence of Cheating

*Logbook 10 · Summer 2026*

A data point sitting far away from the rest looks very convincing on a chart. It catches the eye. It creates the feeling that something unusual has happened and almost offers a story to explain itself. When the data comes from an examination-where every number is tied to someone’s opportunity-the temptation to tell that story becomes even stronger. While analyzing Vietnam’s 2026 high-school examination data, I used several methods to find unusual signals: Z-scores, MAD, IQR, Isolation Forest, LOF, DBSCAN, matched-group comparisons, windows of consecutive candidate numbers, and distributions across subjects or admission combinations. Those methods can flag a student, an examination room, or a cluster of scores. They cannot say, by themselves, “This is cheating.” An anomaly-detection method usually answers something close to: Does this point differ from the pattern the model considers normal? That is not the same as asking: What happened inside the examination room? The distance between those two questions contains many things: data quality, the selected comparison group, selective schools, chosen subjects, test design, examination procedures, entry errors, and variables I cannot observe at all. A genuinely exceptional student is also an outlier. A school with selective admission will have a different distribution. A small group creates unstable variance estimates. A threshold chosen after viewing the data can produce a “signal” more attractive than the truth. The algorithm does not know the context. The analyst is responsible for saying so-especially when the result can damage another person’s reputation.

When a Z-score, Isolation Forest, and LOF all flag the same point, certainty seems to increase. Yet all three may be reacting to the same feature. Three thermometers placed beside one another do not turn temperature into the cause of a fever. I try to separate three layers of evidence: **Description:** distributions, high-score rates, correlations, and national position; **Comparison:** contrast with similar groups based on subjects, observed performance, or context; **Mechanism:** a specific explanation for the pattern, supported by independent evidence that can be checked. Examination-score analysis may handle the first two reasonably well if the data is clean. The third requires information that a table of scores does not contain. The responsible sentence is not “the data proves.” It is “the data shows a signal that requires further verification,” followed by a clear statement of which evidence would verify it. One approach I used was a matched control group: compare students with similar performance in other subjects, then examine the difference in the subject of interest. This is more reasonable than comparing one school directly with the national average. But “similar” means similar only in the variables available. Two students with the same Mathematics and English scores may still differ in curriculum, subject choices, test preparation, or admission goals. Matching reduces some bias. It does not create a randomized experiment.

Whenever a more sophisticated method produces a stronger result, I need to ask in return: which alternative explanation has this method removed, and which ones remain? Model complexity does not release an analyst from the duty to remain humble. If I test many schools, subjects, candidate-number windows, and thresholds, I will eventually find something “statistically significant” through chance alone. If I report only the striking tests, the analysis can tell a powerful story created by the search process itself. A better process should: pre-register the main hypothesis and test when possible; report the number of comparisons; adjust for multiple testing; show effect size and uncertainty, not only a p-value; test sensitivity to thresholds and comparison groups; keep results that do not support the original hypothesis. A small p-value cannot repair a question chosen after the answer was already visible. Educational data analysis is easily pulled toward two extremes. One side wants every strong signal published immediately in the name of fairness. The other argues that if an absolute conclusion is impossible, no analysis should be attempted. I agree with neither. Ignoring unusual patterns may overlook a real problem. Concluding too quickly may harm innocent people. The responsible path lies between them: detect a signal, disclose the method, limit the interpretation, and propose an independent verification process. Language is part of the method. “Unusual,” “difficult to explain,” “requires verification,” and “cheating” cannot be used as interchangeable terms.

Examination data can help prioritize where a review is needed. It can show how rare a distribution is, whether a result survives several comparisons, or how a policy affects different groups. It can also help design a better system: more complete logs, cross-checking procedures, privacy-preserving data publication, and review criteria defined before a controversy begins. That is the role I find appropriate for data science: make the question clearer and make the decision to investigate more transparent. Not act as a judge holding a CSV file. An outlier is an invitation to investigate. It has never been a verdict.
